## 1. Why Fine-Tune at All?

Large language models like Claude Sonnet or GPT-4 are **generalists**. They were trained on a vast slice of human knowledge — books, code, Wikipedia, scientific papers, web pages — so they can hold a conversation, write poetry, debug code, and explain quantum physics all in one session.

That generalist ability is remarkable. It is also expensive.

When you use a large LLM in production to perform a **specific, well-defined task** — classify a support ticket, generate a structured JSON response, call an API in the right format — you are paying generalist prices for specialist work. The model processes millions of parameters it doesn't need for your narrow use case, spends compute reasoning about possibilities that aren't relevant, and charges you for every token of that extra capability.

**Fine-tuning** is the process of taking a smaller, cheaper model and teaching it to perform your specific task extremely well. The result is a model that:

- Costs 5–20× less to run per query
- Responds faster (fewer parameters = less compute)
- Performs as well or better than the large model on that specific task (because it's specialized)

> **Analogy:** Imagine hiring a general management consultant for every task in your company — contract review, marketing, engineering decisions, HR policies. You'd get competent work across the board, but at extraordinary cost. Fine-tuning is like training specialists for each function: the specialist is less expensive, faster, and often better because they're focused.

**When fine-tuning makes sense:**
- The task is well-defined with consistent inputs and outputs
- You have (or can generate) examples of what good outputs look like
- You run many queries per day, making cost reduction meaningful
- Quality requirements are high enough that a general model sometimes falls short

**When fine-tuning does NOT make sense:**
- The task is highly variable or unpredictable
- You need the model to reason across very diverse domains simultaneously
- Query volume is too low to justify the one-time effort
- You're still experimenting with the task definition

## 2. What Is Fine-Tuning? (The Core Idea)

Before exploring specific techniques, it helps to understand what actually happens when a model "learns."

### How Models Store Knowledge

A language model is, at its core, a very large collection of numbers — called **weights** or **parameters**. A small model might have 7 billion of these numbers; a large one might have 100 billion or more. Every time the model generates a word, it performs a calculation using these weights to decide what comes next.

During pre-training, these weights were set over months of compute on massive datasets. The patterns in all that text — grammar, facts, reasoning styles, code syntax — are encoded in those billions of numbers.

### What Fine-Tuning Does

Fine-tuning **continues that learning process** on a much smaller, focused dataset. The model sees new examples, computes how far its current outputs are from what the examples expect, and nudges its weights slightly to reduce that gap. This process repeats thousands of times across the dataset.

The key insight: **fine-tuning doesn't erase what the model already knows.** It shifts the model's behavior in the direction of the new examples without overwriting general capabilities — like adding a specialization on top of a foundation.

> **Analogy:** Think of a general practitioner completing a cardiology residency. They don't forget how to take a patient history or diagnose a cold. They deepen their capability in one specific domain by studying thousands of cardiology cases. Their weights (medical knowledge) shift — they now respond faster and more accurately when they see a cardiology problem.

### The Training Loop (Simplified)

1. Show the model an input (a prompt, a question, a document)
2. The model produces an output based on its current weights
3. Compare the output to the expected answer from the training example
4. Measure the gap (the "loss")
5. Adjust the weights slightly to reduce the gap
6. Repeat for all training examples — this is one "epoch"
7. Repeat for multiple epochs until the model's outputs are consistently close to the expected answers

This is gradient descent — a mathematical technique for finding the downhill direction in the loss landscape and taking a small step toward it with every training example.

## 3. Fine-Tuning Approaches

There are many ways to fine-tune a model, each with different trade-offs in quality, cost, data requirements, and complexity. This section explains all the major approaches so you can make an informed choice.

---

### 3.1 Full Fine-Tuning

**What it is:** Update every single weight in the model on your task-specific data. Nothing is frozen.

**How it works:** The entire model participates in training — every layer, every parameter gets adjusted. This gives the model maximum freedom to adapt to your task.

**The problem:** A 7-billion parameter model requires roughly 14 GB just to store the weights, plus another 40–60 GB for the optimizer state and gradients during training. Few teams have access to the GPU clusters needed to run this. More importantly, updating every parameter aggressively on a small dataset causes **catastrophic forgetting** — the model loses its general capabilities as it over-specializes. Ask it something outside your training distribution and it may fail completely.

**When to use:** Almost never, unless you have a very large task-specific dataset and a team with dedicated ML infrastructure.

> **Analogy:** Redecorating every room in a hotel to match one guest's taste. By the time the guest checks out, the hotel is perfectly suited for them and useless for anyone else.

---

### 3.2 Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

**What it is:** Train the model on a dataset of (input, correct output) pairs. The model learns by imitation.

**How it works:** Each training example is a prompt with a corresponding expected response. The model is trained to produce the expected response given the prompt, using standard next-token prediction loss. This is the most common and foundational fine-tuning technique.

**Strengths:** Straightforward, well-understood, and natively supported by most platforms including AWS Bedrock. If you have high-quality labeled examples, SFT will reliably shift the model toward producing similar outputs.

**Limitations:** The model learns to imitate the training distribution. It doesn't learn to actively prefer good outputs over bad ones — it learns what the correct answer looks like from examples, but it has no concept of "this answer is better than that answer." Rare or edge-case examples that appear infrequently in training data will be under-learned.

> **Analogy:** Giving a student a textbook filled with worked problems and asking them to study it. They learn by observing what correct answers look like. They'll do well on problems similar to the textbook examples; they may struggle with problems that look different.

---

### 3.3 Continued Pre-Training (CPT)

**What it is:** Keep training the model on raw, unlabeled domain text — no correct answers provided, just text to read.

**How it works:** The model continues its original pre-training process on domain-specific documents (technical documentation, internal wikis, domain-specific reports). It learns the vocabulary, writing style, and patterns of that domain without being told what "good" outputs look like.

**Use case:** Best as a preparatory step before SFT when your domain uses highly specialized language that the base model may not have seen much of during pre-training. For example: legal documents, medical records, proprietary engineering jargon.

**Limitation:** CPT alone does not teach the model to perform a task. It enriches its vocabulary and context but provides no direction on what to generate. Always follow CPT with SFT if you need task-specific behavior.

> **Analogy:** Sending a new hire to read a year's worth of your company's internal documentation before they start. They'll understand the culture, terminology, and context — but they still need training on how to do their actual job.

---

### 3.4 LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)

**What it is:** Freeze the entire base model and instead train tiny "adapter" matrices that are injected alongside specific layers.

**How it works:** LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is based on a mathematical insight: during fine-tuning, the changes made to model weights are empirically **low-rank** — meaning they can be represented as the product of two much smaller matrices. Instead of updating the full weight matrix (e.g., 4,096 × 4,096 = 16.7M parameters), LoRA trains two small matrices (e.g., 4,096 × 16 and 16 × 4,096 = 131K parameters total) whose product approximates the update. The base model stays frozen; only the adapter matrices receive gradient updates.

**Result:** A LoRA adapter file is typically 10–200 MB, compared to the 14 GB of the base 7B model. Multiple adapters for different tasks can share the same frozen base model, swapped at inference time. Training is faster and requires far less GPU memory.

**Important for Bedrock:** LoRA adapters must be **merged** into the base model weights before being imported into Bedrock via Custom Model Import. Bedrock does not serve raw adapter files directly. LoRA is NOT natively part of Bedrock's own fine-tuning pipeline.

> **Analogy:** Attaching a specialized lens to a camera instead of buying a new camera body for each shot. The camera body (base model) is unchanged and can be used with any lens. The lens (LoRA adapter) adds a specific optical character for that shot.

---

### 3.5 QLoRA (Quantized LoRA)

**What it is:** LoRA, but the frozen base model is stored in compressed (quantized) format to dramatically reduce GPU memory requirements.

**How it works:** The base model weights are stored in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) format instead of the standard 16-bit format — a 75% reduction in memory. The LoRA adapter matrices are still trained in full 16-bit precision (they are tiny, so memory is not a concern for them). During training, the 4-bit weights are temporarily converted back to 16-bit for computation and then discarded.

**Result:** A 7B model that normally requires 14 GB of GPU memory to fine-tune now requires approximately 4 GB. This makes fine-tuning feasible on a single mid-tier GPU on Amazon SageMaker.

**Trade-off:** There is a small quality penalty from quantization. For structured output tasks (generating JSON, calling APIs), this penalty is typically negligible. For tasks requiring nuanced creative reasoning, it may be slightly more noticeable.

Like standard LoRA, QLoRA requires SageMaker and Bedrock Custom Model Import — it is not natively part of Bedrock's managed pipeline.

> **Analogy:** The same specialized lens, but manufactured from lighter materials that reduce weight by 75% with a trivially small reduction in optical precision.

---

### 3.6 RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)

**What it is:** The technique behind the alignment of ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini. Trains a model to maximize human preference scores.

**How it works:** Three stages:

1. **SFT** — teach the model with labeled examples to get a reasonable starting point
2. **Reward model training** — collect human comparisons ("was response A or response B better?") and train a separate neural network (the reward model) to predict human preference scores
3. **Policy optimization (PPO)** — use reinforcement learning (specifically Proximal Policy Optimization) to train the language model to produce outputs that score highly according to the reward model, while not drifting too far from the SFT starting point

**The appeal:** RLHF can teach models to satisfy nuanced human preferences that are hard to express as labeled examples — tone, helpfulness, honesty, safety.

**The reality in practice:** RLHF requires training and maintaining two models simultaneously. PPO training is notoriously unstable — small hyperparameter changes can cause training to collapse. The reward model can be "gamed" by the policy model (it learns to produce outputs that fool the reward model rather than outputs that are genuinely better). Setting up this pipeline requires deep ML expertise.

**Bedrock support:** AWS Bedrock does not natively offer RLHF. This is a compelling reason to consider alternatives (see RFT below).

> **Analogy:** Hiring a professional food critic to score every dish a chef produces, then training the chef to maximize the critic's scores. The critic adds expert judgment but also costs a great deal, can be inconsistent, and the chef may learn to "cook for the critic" rather than for the diner.

---

### 3.7 DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)

**What it is:** A mathematically equivalent alternative to RLHF that eliminates the reward model and the RL training loop entirely.

**How it works:** DPO takes a key insight: if you work backward from what the optimal RLHF-trained policy would look like, you can derive a direct training objective on (chosen, rejected) output pairs without ever training a reward model. The loss function directly encourages the model to assign higher probability to chosen responses and lower probability to rejected responses, anchored against the SFT model to prevent the model from drifting too far.

**What you need:** For each training prompt, you need a pair: one output labeled "chosen" (better) and one labeled "rejected" (worse). Generating these pairs is the main data challenge — the rejected examples need to be plausible enough that the model might actually produce them, otherwise the signal is trivial.

**Strengths over RLHF:** No reward model to train. Stable offline training (the dataset is static, not generated online). Training looks almost identical to SFT from an infrastructure perspective — just gradient descent on a static dataset.

**Bedrock support:** DPO is NOT natively supported on Bedrock. It requires training on Amazon SageMaker and then importing the resulting model into Bedrock via Custom Model Import.

> **Analogy:** Instead of hiring a critic to score every dish in real time, you compile a reference book of "dishes that were praised" vs. "dishes that were returned" and teach the chef using that book directly. No ongoing critic needed — the signal is already baked into the training data.

---

### 3.8 RFT (Reinforcement Fine-Tuning) — AWS Bedrock Native

**What it is:** AWS Bedrock's native reward-based learning technique, added in December 2025. Achieves preference alignment without labeled (chosen, rejected) pairs and without the complexity of RLHF.

**How it works:** You define a **scoring function** — either a Lambda function that runs a deterministic check on the model's output, or an AI-based judge that evaluates quality. For each training prompt, the model generates multiple candidate responses; the scoring function rates all of them; the model is trained to produce outputs that score higher in future attempts. This training loop uses **GRPO** (Group Relative Policy Optimization), a stable algorithm that doesn't require a separate reward model or PPO's complex training dynamics.

**The key advantage:** You don't need pre-labeled "good" and "bad" examples. You just need a function that can tell good from bad at inference time. For tasks where correctness is programmably verifiable — valid JSON, a passing unit test, a successful API call, a factually accurate calculation — RFT is extremely powerful.

**Is RFT required after distillation?** No. Distillation alone is a complete, production-ready fine-tuning strategy. RFT is an optional enhancement to add only if your post-distillation evaluation reveals the model falling short on tasks where you can verify correctness with a function. Think of distillation as getting you 80–90% of the way to your quality goal; RFT refines the remaining 10–20% for programmable tasks.

**Bedrock support:** Natively supported on Amazon Nova 2 Lite, OpenAI GPT-OSS 20B, and Qwen3 32B (model catalog growing).

> **Analogy:** Instead of providing labeled examples of good and bad cooking, you give the chef access to a panel of diners and let them experiment — the chef tries different variations of a dish, diners score each one, and the chef learns from the scores. No reference cookbook needed; just a feedback mechanism.

---

### 3.9 ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization)

**What it is:** A newer research technique (2024) that merges SFT and preference alignment into a single training pass.

**How it works:** Standard DPO requires two stages: first SFT, then DPO on top of the SFT checkpoint. ORPO adds a preference alignment term directly into the SFT loss function. In a single training pass, the model simultaneously learns to produce the chosen response (via standard next-token prediction loss) and to avoid the rejected response (via an odds ratio penalty). No reference model is needed — this simplifies the training setup and reduces memory usage.

**Status:** ORPO is not yet natively supported on AWS Bedrock. It requires SageMaker training and Custom Model Import if you want to use it. It is mentioned here as an indicator of where the field is moving: toward simpler, single-stage alignment training.

---

### 3.10 Model Distillation

**What it is:** Transfer the intelligence of a large, expensive model (the **teacher**) into a small, cheap model (the **student**) by having the teacher generate training data that the student learns from.

**How it works:** The process has three steps:
1. You provide a set of prompts relevant to your use case
2. The teacher model answers all those prompts — producing high-quality, detailed responses
3. The student model is fine-tuned on those (prompt, teacher response) pairs

The student learns to produce outputs similar in quality to the teacher on that domain. Since the teacher's responses often include explicit reasoning, the student implicitly learns *how* to reason about the problem, not just *what* to say.

**Why this is particularly powerful on AWS Bedrock:** Bedrock's distillation pipeline is fully managed. You don't need to write data generation code, manage training infrastructure, or handle the SFT job yourself. You provide prompts (or point at existing production invocation logs), and Bedrock:
- Sends those prompts to the teacher model
- Applies proprietary data synthesis techniques to generate diverse, high-quality variations (up to 15K prompt-response pairs)
- Splits data into training and validation sets
- Fine-tunes the student model
- Reports validation metrics

If your system already uses the large LLM in production and has **invocation logging enabled**, you can feed real production logs directly into a distillation job — the teacher's actual responses to your users' actual inputs become the training corpus. This is the most data-efficient path available.

> **Analogy:** A senior chef (teacher) has spent decades perfecting their craft. They sit down, record every recipe, explain every technique, and annotate why they make each decision. A junior chef (student) studies this library intensively. The junior chef doesn't have the senior chef's lifetime of experience — but for the dishes covered in the library, they can cook nearly as well, much faster, and at a fraction of the cost.

## 5. Approach Comparison

With the accurate Bedrock landscape established, here is how the major fine-tuning strategies compare for a team looking to replace a large, expensive LLM with a fine-tuned SLM:

| | Plain SFT | Bedrock Distillation | Distillation + RFT | DPO via SageMaker |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Quality** | Good baseline | Better — teacher's reasoning transfers | Best on verifiable tasks | Highest ceiling on any task |
| **Bedrock native** | ✅ Fully | ✅ Fully | ✅ Fully | ❌ Requires SageMaker |
| **Training data needed** | Manually prepared (input, output) pairs | Prompts only OR production invocation logs | Prompts + a scoring function (Lambda) | (chosen, rejected) pairs per prompt |
| **Data preparation burden** | Medium | **Very low** | Low + scoring function | High |
| **Operational complexity** | Low | Low | Low to Medium | High |
| **Is RFT mandatory after distillation?** | N/A | **No — distillation is complete on its own** | Optional, only if quality gap remains | N/A |
| **When to choose this** | You already have clean labeled data | Starting point for most teams | Distillation quality not sufficient on programmable tasks | Need a model not in Bedrock's catalog |

### On "Is RFT mandatory after distillation?"

**No.** Distillation alone is a complete, production-ready approach. The student model learns the teacher's output patterns including its reasoning, format adherence, and domain vocabulary. RFT is an optional second pass — only worth pursuing if post-distillation evaluation shows a quality gap on tasks where output correctness can be measured programmatically (correct JSON structure, a successful API call, a passing test). If the distilled model meets your quality bar, ship it and move on.

### On "Is SageMaker + DPO worth it?"

For most teams adopting fine-tuning on Bedrock for the first time: **no.** Here is the honest assessment:

- DPO requires constructing (chosen, rejected) output pairs. Generating useful rejected examples is harder than it sounds — they need to be plausible (the model might actually produce them) but clearly worse than the chosen examples.
- SageMaker adds a training pipeline you need to manage, monitor, and update. The resulting model requires LoRA merging, safetensors conversion, and Custom Model Import — multiple steps with failure points.
- Bedrock's native Distillation + optional RFT achieves the same quality improvement landscape for structured and programmable tasks without any of this overhead.

**When SageMaker DPO genuinely adds value:**
1. You need a model family (e.g., Mistral 7B, Phi-4, specific Qwen variants) that isn't available in Bedrock's native fine-tuning catalog
2. You have already collected a large corpus of human preference pairs from your production system (e.g., from user feedback on model outputs) and want to leverage them
3. You are building a research or benchmarking system and need the most fine-grained control over every aspect of training

For standard production use cases — replacing an expensive LLM with a cheaper, task-specialized SLM — start Bedrock-native and graduate to SageMaker only when you hit a hard constraint.

## 6. Recommended Approach: Start with Bedrock Distillation

### Why Distillation First

Bedrock Distillation is the strongest starting point for most teams because:

1. **It requires almost no data preparation.** If your current system logs Bedrock invocations, those logs are the training data — no labeling, no JSONL construction, no data pipeline.
2. **It transfers the teacher's reasoning, not just its answers.** When the teacher model includes a reasoning chain in its response, the student learns to reason similarly — a form of chain-of-thought distillation that plain SFT from human-labeled data cannot replicate as easily.
3. **It is fully managed.** Bedrock runs the teacher model, synthesizes diverse examples, handles the training job, validates the model, and stores the result. No infrastructure to provision or manage.
4. **Quality is anchored to the teacher.** The distilled student model is explicitly trained to match the teacher's performance. This is a higher-quality starting point than training from scratch on human-labeled examples, which are often inconsistent or incomplete.

### The Recommended Step-by-Step

```
Step 1: Enable invocation logging
Step 2: Create a distillation job
Step 3: Evaluate the distilled model
Step 4 (optional): Apply RFT if needed
Step 5: Shadow mode before full rollout
```

---

#### Step 1 — Enable Invocation Logging

Turn on Bedrock invocation logging for the system currently using the large LLM. Every prompt sent to the teacher and every response it generates is written to a CloudWatch log group or an S3 bucket. Let this run for a representative period (a few weeks is usually enough to capture the range of inputs your system sees in production).

If invocation logging is already enabled, you have training data ready — no additional work needed before Step 2.

---

#### Step 2 — Create a Distillation Job

In the Bedrock console (or via API):
- **Teacher model:** the large model currently powering your system (e.g., Claude Sonnet 4, Llama 3.1 405B)
- **Student model:** a compatible smaller model from the supported pairs table (e.g., Claude 3.5 Haiku, Llama 3.1 8B)
- **Training data source:** either your invocation logs (preferred — real production data) or a set of representative prompts
- **Optional:** provide a handful of labeled "golden examples" — prompt-response pairs that represent ideal outputs — to guide the teacher's response generation style

Bedrock takes it from there: it sends your prompts to the teacher, applies data synthesis to generate diverse variations, splits the data into training and validation sets, runs the fine-tuning job, and produces a custom model endpoint.

---

#### Step 3 — Evaluate the Distilled Model

Before routing any production traffic, test the distilled student on a **held-out evaluation set** — a collection of examples that were not included in the training data. This set should reflect the real range of inputs your system handles, including edge cases.

Measure what matters for your task:
- **Format compliance:** does the model reliably produce output in the expected structure?
- **Task accuracy:** on examples with known correct answers, does the model get them right?
- **Latency:** how much faster is the student vs. the teacher?
- **Cost per query:** calculate the reduction in token cost at your expected query volume

Compare each metric against the teacher model's baseline on the same evaluation set. Define a minimum quality threshold (e.g., "student must match teacher within 5 percentage points on accuracy, and be at least 4× cheaper per query") before proceeding.

---

#### Step 4 (Optional) — Apply RFT if Quality Gap Remains

If the distilled student falls short of your quality threshold on tasks where output correctness can be **programmatically verified**, add a Reinforcement Fine-Tuning pass:

- Write a Lambda function that scores a model output on a 0–1 scale
  - Examples: 1.0 for syntactically valid JSON matching the required schema; 0.0 for any deviation
  - Examples: 1.0 for a successful API call with the generated parameters; 0.0 for a 4xx error
- Submit an RFT job on Bedrock pointing at your prompts and the scoring function
- The model iterates: generating outputs, scoring them, and adjusting toward higher-scoring outputs

RFT is specifically powerful when you can write a deterministic scoring function. If your quality concern is subjective (tone, naturalness of language, helpfulness) and cannot be captured by a function, RFT is less useful — the distilled model's performance on those dimensions is better improved by adding more diverse training prompts to Step 2.

**RFT is not a replacement for distillation.** It is an enhancement layer, run on top of an already-distilled model, for specific programmable quality dimensions.

---

#### Step 5 — Shadow Mode Before Full Rollout

Do not switch production traffic to the fine-tuned model all at once. Instead, run the models in parallel:

1. **Shadow mode:** Route 100% of traffic to the original teacher model for production. Also send the same inputs to the fine-tuned student model in parallel (asynchronously, without affecting the user). Compare outputs and log disagreements.
2. **Canary rollout:** Once you have confidence in shadow mode results, route a small percentage (e.g., 5–10%) of real traffic to the student model. Monitor error rates, output quality signals, and any downstream failures.
3. **Gradual increase:** Expand the student's traffic share to 50%, then 100%, pausing at each stage to confirm quality holds.
4. **Automatic fallback:** Configure the system to automatically return to the teacher model for any individual query where the student's output fails a quality check (invalid format, low confidence signal). This means the student's failures are invisible to users.

## 7. Failure Modes and How to Prevent Them

Fine-tuned SLMs can fail in ways that a large general model rarely does. Knowing these risks upfront makes them preventable.

| What can go wrong | Why it happens | How to prevent it |
|---|---|---|
| **Output format breaks downstream code** | Smaller model produces malformed JSON, an unexpected field name, or a truncated response that can't be parsed | Validate every output against a schema before acting on it. Route failures automatically to the original large model. Never let a format error propagate downstream. |
| **Model misses rare but important cases** | Rare inputs appear infrequently in training data; the model learns majority patterns and applies them to minority cases | Identify rare but high-stakes input types and deliberately include representative examples in your distillation prompts. Optionally use RFT with a reward function that gives extra weight to correctly handling rare inputs. |
| **Model forgets general language capability** | Aggressive SFT on a narrow task can narrow the model's output distribution so much it fails on unusual phrasing of the same task | Distillation from a strong teacher preserves more general capability than manual SFT (the teacher's responses include natural variation). Monitor on diverse test cases, including unusual phrasings. |
| **Model becomes stale as your world changes** | Fine-tuning captures a snapshot of your task at training time. As your product evolves, new input types emerge that the model has never seen | Treat fine-tuning as a recurring process. Re-run distillation periodically with fresh invocation logs. Set a calendar reminder to re-evaluate the student model every quarter. |
| **Model is confidently wrong** | Smaller models may produce an incorrect answer with apparent high confidence, with no signal that it is uncertain | Add a confidence signal to the model's output (instruct it to include a confidence score, or measure output probability if the API supports it). Set a threshold: below a confidence level, automatically fall back to the original large model for that query. |
| **Training data quality degrades** | If the teacher model has been making errors (hallucinations, format failures, incorrect answers) and those errors are in the invocation logs, the student learns to reproduce them | Inspect a random sample of invocation logs before using them for distillation. Filter out logs where the teacher's response is known to be incorrect (e.g., responses that led to downstream errors). |

## Summary

| Topic | Key Takeaway |
|---|---|
| Why fine-tune | Replace expensive generalist LLMs with cheap, specialized SLMs for specific, well-defined tasks |
| Core mechanic | Fine-tuning shifts model weights toward the training distribution; it does not erase prior knowledge |
| Most important concept | Model Distillation — transfer a large model's quality to a small model using production logs; Bedrock automates the entire pipeline |
| Best starting approach | Bedrock native distillation: enable invocation logging → distillation job → evaluate → optional RFT → shadow rollout |
| Is RFT mandatory? | No — distillation alone is often production-ready. Add RFT only if the quality gap on programmable tasks remains after distillation. |
| Is SageMaker DPO worth it? | Not for most teams. Start Bedrock-native. Use SageMaker only when Bedrock's model catalog doesn't contain the model you need. |
| Biggest operational risk | Deploying the student model without a fallback mechanism. Always validate outputs and fall back to the teacher on failure. |
